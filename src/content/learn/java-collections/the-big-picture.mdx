<Part id="what" heading="Job [descriptions], not people" sub="Interfaces say what. Classes say how." />

Picture a notice on a shop door. *Wanted: someone to keep the list of visitors. Must be able to add a name, check if a name is there, and say how many are on the list.* The notice says what the job is. It says nothing about who does it. A student, a retired teacher or a very organised parrot could all fill it, as long as they can do those three things.

Java's collections are built the same way. A **job description** is called an **interface**. It is a list of methods, with names but no working-out inside. The **people** who fill the job are **classes**. A class that does the job is said to **implement** the interface. `List` is an interface. `ArrayList` is one class that implements it. `LinkedList` is another. Both can do everything the `List` notice asks for, in their own way.

Most of the framework comes down to four jobs.

- **`List`**: items in order, repeats allowed, found by position. Think of a queue of names in a register.
- **`Set`**: items with no repeats. Think of a guest list where nobody is invited twice.
- **`Queue`**: items waiting their turn. The first to join is the first to be served.
- **`Map`**: pairs of a **key** and a **value**, where you look up the value by its key. Think of a phone book: name in, number out.

`List`, `Set` and `Queue` share a parent job called **`Collection`**: a group of items you can add to, remove from, count and loop over. `Map` does not share it, because it holds pairs rather than single items.

The habit this lesson wants you to keep is simple. **Write your code against the interface, and choose the class in one place.** You will see why in a few minutes.

**Remember:** an interface is the job description, a class is the person. Ask for the job, not the person.

<InterfacesDiagram />

The solid boxes are the interfaces. `Collection` sits on top, with `List`, `Set` and `Queue` underneath. Under each one are two of the classes you will learn in this course. `Map` stands apart with its own classes.

Above `Collection` there is one more interface, `Iterable`, which is the promise that lets you use a for-each loop on something. You will meet it properly in lesson 5, so we leave it out of the picture for now.

A few things to notice.

- `ArrayList` and `LinkedList` do the same job, `List`, in different ways. Choosing between them is lesson 3 and 4.
- `HashSet` and `TreeSet` both refuse repeats, but only one of them keeps its items sorted.
- `ArrayDeque` and `PriorityQueue` are both queues, but the second one serves the *smallest* item first, not the oldest.
- `LinkedList` also appears under `Queue`, but we have drawn it only once. It does several jobs.

You don't have to memorise the tree. You have to know that each box is a job, and that the dashed boxes are the people who can do it.

Here is each job in action. Every block below can be pasted into jshell. The `<String>` part just means "a list of strings". Lesson 2 explains it fully.

**A list** keeps order and allows repeats.

<Snippet file="Lesson1BigPicture" name="list-demo" />

```text
[Asha, Ravi, Asha]
Ravi
3
```

Positions start at **0**, so `get(1)` is the second item. That is why it printed `Ravi`.

**A set** refuses repeats.

<Snippet file="Lesson1BigPicture" name="set-demo" />

```text
false
2
true
```

The second `add("Asha")` did nothing, and told us so by returning `false`. A `HashSet` also makes no promise about the order of its items, which is why we only ask it for its size and whether it contains something.

**A queue** serves the longest-waiting item first.

<Snippet file="Lesson1BigPicture" name="queue-demo" />

```text
Asha
Ravi
2
```

`offer` joins the back of the line. `poll` takes the person at the front and hands them to you. `peek` only looks.

**A map** answers "what goes with this key?"

<Snippet file="Lesson1BigPicture" name="map-demo" />

```text
90
null
0
2
```

Putting "Asha" a second time replaced her mark, so the map still has two entries. Asking for a key that isn't there gives `null`, which means "nothing here". `getOrDefault` lets you pick the answer to use instead.

<Part id="inside" heading="Why the [interface] on the left" sub="Swap the class later, change one line." />

When you call `names.add("Asha")`, Java looks at the real object behind `names` and runs *its* version of `add`. If the object is an `ArrayList`, you get the `ArrayList` way. If it is a `LinkedList`, you get that way. This is called **polymorphism**, a long word for "the same call, different behaviour depending on the object". It is what makes the habit work.

Two honest cautions follow.

**The interface does not promise speed.** `List.get(5)` is instant on an `ArrayList` and slow on a `LinkedList`. Both are correct `List`s. The interface only promises the *behaviour*. The cost belongs to the class, and the class pages in this course give you the cost tables.

**Some jobs are filled only in part.** A list made with `List.of(...)` is a real `List`, but it cannot change, so `add` on it throws an `UnsupportedOperationException`. The interface lists `add`, but says it is *optional* for a class to support it. That is a quirk to be aware of. We come back to it in lesson 10.

Finally, why is `Map` not a `Collection`? A `Collection` is a group of single items. A `Map` is a group of pairs, so methods like `add(item)` have no sensible meaning for it. It is still part of the framework. You can ask a map for its keys, its values, or its entries, and each of those is a `Collection` or a `Set`.

<Part id="cost" heading="Which job, which [cost]" sub="n is the number of items." />

The four jobs answer four different questions. A typical class for each one is shown with what it is quick at.

| Job | Question it answers | Typical class | Quick at |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `List` | What is at position 5? | `ArrayList` | Get by position: O(1) |
| `Set` | Have I seen this before? | `HashSet` | Contains: O(1) on average |
| `Queue` | Who is next? | `ArrayDeque` | Add at back, take from front: O(1) |
| `Map` | What goes with this key? | `HashMap` | Get by key: O(1) on average |

"On average" matters for hashing. A `HashSet` or `HashMap` is quick in the usual case, and lesson 6 explains the rare case where it is not.

Notice what each class is *not* quick at. Asking an `ArrayList` "do you contain Ravi?" means looking at items one by one, which is O(n). Lesson 11 turns this table into a full guide for choosing.

<Part id="interview" heading="The first [question]" sub="What the interviewer is really asking." />

Questions about the framework's shape come early in a Java interview. They are easy to answer if you keep the job-and-person picture in your head.

- **"Why `List<String> x = new ArrayList<>()` and not `ArrayList<String> x = ...`?"** Because the variable should state what job you need, and the right side states who does it. Then you can change the class in one place, and methods that take a `List` accept any kind.
- **"Is `Map` a `Collection`?"** No. `Collection` is for single items, and `Map` holds key and value pairs. They are separate branches of the framework.
- **"What is the difference between `Collection` and `Collections`?"** `Collection` is the interface at the top of the tree. `Collections`, with an s, is a class full of helper methods such as sorting and reversing. Lesson 10 covers it.
- **"List or Set?"** Do you need repeats and positions, or do you need each item at most once? Say what you need before you say a class name.
- **"What does a queue's `poll` do when the queue is empty?"** It returns `null`. The method `remove` on a queue throws a `NoSuchElementException` instead. Knowing that both exist, and which one is the gentle one, shows you've used them.
- **Say the cost with the class.** "A `List` gives me get-by-position, but whether that is O(1) depends on whether it is an `ArrayList` or a `LinkedList`." That sentence shows you know an interface makes no promise about speed.
