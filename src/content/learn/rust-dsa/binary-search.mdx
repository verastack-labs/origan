<Part id="what" heading="Finding a word in a [dictionary]" sub="Open in the middle, and throw half away." />

You want the word "pelican" in a printed dictionary. You do not start at page one. You open it near the middle and see "mongoose". Pelican comes after that, so you ignore the entire first half. Now you open the middle of what is left, see "rabbit", and ignore the back half. A few flips later, you are on the right page.

That is **binary search**. Each look at the middle throws away half of what remains. It only works because a dictionary is **sorted**, so one look tells you which half to keep.

How fast is it? A dictionary of 1,000 pages takes about 10 looks. A million pages takes about 20. A billion takes about 30. Compare that with reading page by page. This is the speed of the idea, and it is why sorting (lesson 13) is worth doing first.

Reach for it when the data is sorted and you keep asking "where is this?". It also works in a quieter way, on a problem where the *answer* is a number and you can test whether a guess is too small or big enough. That second use is the one interviewers love.

<Part id="idea" heading="Keep the part still in [play]" sub="Two edges, one middle, one decision." />

<BinarySearchDiagram />

Try it on eleven sorted numbers, hunting for 45. Keep track of the part still in play with two edges, `lo` on the left and `hi` on the right.

Look at the middle: 23. It is smaller than 45, so 45 cannot be in 23's half. Everything from 23 leftward is out. The left edge moves to just past the middle. Look at the middle of what is left: 56. Too big, so the right edge moves in. Look again: 45. Found, after three looks.

The pattern never changes. Look at the middle, compare, and move one edge. The picture shows the faded boxes that are ruled out.

Now try it yourself. Pick a number to hunt for and press Next. Try the ends, and try 40, which is not in the row, to see what happens when the edges meet.

<BinarySearchStepper />

Two details trip people up, so get them right now.

**Is the right edge in or out?** In this lesson `hi` is one past the end, just like Rust's ranges `lo..hi`. The search runs while `lo < hi`. Pick one convention and stick to it. Most binary search bugs are an edge that is off by one.

**What if it is not there?** Then the edges meet. The part in play shrinks to nothing, and `lo` ends up pointing at the gap where the item *would* go. That is a useful free gift, as you will see.

**Remember:** look at the middle, throw half away, and keep your edge rules the same on every step.

Here it is for a sorted slice of numbers.

Go through it in order.

- The part in play is `lo..hi`. At the start that is the whole slice.
- `mid` is the middle of it. Writing `lo + (hi - lo) / 2` instead of `(lo + hi) / 2` is a habit worth having. With huge numbers, `lo + hi` can overflow before the division. It cannot overflow here, but the habit stops the bug in other languages.
- If the middle is the target, you are done. If it is too small, `lo = mid + 1`, because the middle itself has been checked and is wrong. If it is too big, `hi = mid`, because `hi` is one past the end and so is already excluded.
- When `lo` meets `hi`, nothing is left in play, and the answer is `None`.

The demo at the bottom also tries the first item, the last item, and a number that is missing. Edges are where the bugs live, so always test them.

There is a second way to use binary search, and it is the one interviewers love. When the answer is a number and you can test a guess, you can binary search over the *numbers themselves*. You need one thing: the test must go from "no, no, no" to "yes, yes, yes", and never flip back. Then the smallest "yes" can be found by halving.

<Snippet file="lesson14_binary_search" name="first-true" />

`first_true` does not look at a slice. It looks at a range of numbers, and asks `ok(mid)`. If `mid` passes, the answer is `mid` or something smaller, so `hi = mid`. If it fails, the answer is bigger, so `lo = mid + 1`. Here it finds the smallest whole number whose square is at least 1000, without trying all of them.

**Remember:** if "is this guess big enough?" has a clean yes or no, you can binary search the answer.

<Part id="std" heading="What Rust already [has]" sub="binary_search and partition_point." />

A slice has `binary_search` built in.

<Snippet file="lesson14_binary_search" name="std-binary-search" />

`binary_search` gives back a `Result`. `Ok(position)` means found. `Err(position)` means not found, and the position is the gap where it would go to keep the slice sorted. When the slice has repeated values, it may return *any* of them. That is rarely what you want, so reach for `partition_point` instead.

`partition_point(|x| test)` assumes the slice starts with a run of items that pass the test and then switches to items that fail. It returns how many pass. In the example, `x < 30` passes for 10 and 20, so the answer is 2, the position of the first 30. `x <= 30` passes for five items, so the answer is 5, the position just after the last 30. Subtract the two and you have counted the 30s, with no loop.

The `Err` side is also how you insert into a sorted `Vec` and keep it sorted.

<Snippet file="lesson14_binary_search" name="insert-sorted" />

If your data is a list of pairs sorted by their first part, `binary_search_by_key` does the same job on that part.

<Snippet file="lesson14_binary_search" name="std-search-by-key" />

All of these need the slice to be sorted in the order you search. On an unsorted slice they return nonsense, with no warning.

**Remember:** `binary_search` for "is it there?", `partition_point` for "where does it go, and how many?".

<Part id="cost" heading="What each search [costs]" sub="n is how many items there are." />

| Operation | Time | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Find by scanning | O(n) | You may look at every item |
| Find by binary search | O(log n) | Each look halves what is left |
| `partition_point` | O(log n) | The same halving |
| Sort first, then search once | O(n log n) | The sort costs more than the search |
| Sort once, then search many times | O(n log n + q log n) | q searches, each O(log n) |
| Insert into a sorted `Vec` | O(n) | Finding the gap is fast, but the items after it must shift |
| Space used | O(1) | Two edges and a middle |

If you only search once, a plain scan can beat sorting first. Binary search pays off when you search the same data many times.

<Part id="interview" heading="Binary search in the [interview]" sub="What to ask, and what to say." />

Binary search is simple to explain and surprisingly easy to get wrong. Interviewers know that, and they watch for the details.

- **Spot the signals.** "Sorted", "find the first or last", "minimum that works", "maximum that still fits", and "in O(log n)" all point here. If a brute force would try every number in a range, ask whether the tries could be halved.
- **Say the invariant.** Before you code, say what is always true: "the answer is always inside `lo..hi`". Then check that every move keeps it true.
- **Choose your edge rules once.** Say whether `hi` is included or one past the end, and write every line to match. A mixed-up pair of rules is the most common bug.
- **Test the edges.** Try an empty slice, one item, the first item, the last item, and a missing item. Say you will do that, and then do it.
- **Know the lookups.** "Where would this go?" is the first position that is not smaller. "How many equal?" is two such positions subtracted. In Rust, that is `partition_point`.
- **Use it when it is not obviously a search.** Questions about "the smallest capacity", "the slowest speed" or "the largest size" are often a search on the answer. The test is a clean yes or no, and the cost is O(log range) tests.
- **Sort cost matters.** If the data is not sorted, sorting first costs O(n log n). For one lookup, a scan is faster. Mention that trade-off.
